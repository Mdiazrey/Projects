# Rate Your Ride: Rethinking Ride-Share Feedback

An HCI design project exploring how ride-share platforms could collect clearer, more contextual, and more useful feedback from riders.

> **Academic group project:** This concept was created for a university course and is not affiliated with or endorsed by Uber or Lyft.

## Overview

Traditional five-star ride-share ratings provide very little context. Riders may interpret four stars as a positive score even though a driver's platform standing can depend on maintaining a much higher average. Ratings can also be influenced by social pressure, unclear expectations, recency, and factors outside a driver's control.

Our team investigated how a rating experience could reduce ambiguity while gathering feedback that is more actionable for drivers and more meaningful for the platform. The final concept replaces abstract stars with labeled choices, introduces optional contextual prompts during and after a ride, and gives riders opportunities to explain or revise their feedback.

## Design process

### 1. Research

The team reviewed existing research about ride-share ratings, algorithmic management, rating bias, and the pressures experienced by gig-economy workers. We then conducted five interviews with a mix of riders and drivers to understand how people interpret ratings and how the system affects behavior.

Recurring themes included:

- Riders often consider a four-star rating positive, while platforms may treat it as damaging.
- Drivers receive limited explanation for low scores and may not know what to improve.
- Riders sometimes avoid honest ratings because they feel socially uncomfortable.
- Feedback collected long after an event is less accurate and less actionable.
- Safety, cleanliness, driving behavior, and the overall experience are distinct concerns but are often compressed into one score.

### 2. Problem definition

We focused on three connected problems:

1. **Ambiguous ratings:** Star values do not communicate consistent expectations.
2. **Low-quality feedback:** A single score provides little guidance to drivers.
3. **Poor timing:** End-of-ride ratings depend on memory and may miss important moments.

Our design goal became: **make feedback quick enough to complete, clear enough to interpret, and specific enough to be useful.**

### 3. Ideation and prototyping

Early concepts explored rider-driver preference matching, slider-based ratings, labeled buttons, mid-ride prompts, and optional follow-up questions. Sketches and intermediate prototypes helped the team compare the effort, clarity, and potential bias introduced by each approach.

The labeled-button system became the primary direction because it required less effort than a slider and encouraged riders to think about the meaning of their selection rather than defaulting to a familiar star value.

### 4. Evaluation and iteration

The team conducted four usability evaluations across the evolving prototypes. Testing led to several concrete changes:

- Changed the highest label from **Perfect** to **Excellent** because participants considered “perfect” too extreme.
- Added clearer explanations of what each rating means.
- Kept additional written feedback optional to reduce effort.
- Added a way to revisit missed questions after the ride.
- Explored undo or confirmation behavior to prevent accidental lock-screen responses.
- Clarified how rider feedback could affect driver safety and performance information.
- Reduced notification burden after participants raised concerns about interruption and fatigue.

## Final concept

The final design includes:

- A five-option labeled scale: **Horrible, Bad, Good, Great, Excellent**.
- Optional written feedback following the overall rating.
- Contextual questions about cleanliness, hospitality, and driving safety.
- Lightweight prompts designed for relevant moments rather than one long survey.
- A post-ride questionnaire for unanswered or revised responses.
- A concept for weighting recent rides more heavily so feedback better reflects current performance.

## Key takeaways

- Familiar interaction patterns are not necessarily well understood.
- Labels can communicate consequences more clearly than abstract stars.
- Context improves feedback quality, but excessive prompts create fatigue.
- Low-effort interactions still require safeguards against accidental input.
- Showing how feedback is used may make users more willing to provide thoughtful responses.

## Limitations and next steps

This was an academic concept evaluated with a small convenience sample. A future iteration should include more riders and active drivers, accessibility testing, investigation of rating bias across demographic groups, and validation with realistic ride contexts. The notification strategy and proposed recent-ride weighting would also require longer-term testing.
